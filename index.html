Every Worlds roster since 2011, and the paths between them.

An interactive map of every team that has played the League of Legends World Championship. Each team is a column, each Worlds a row, and each player a thread coloured by role: follow a career from one team to the next, or a team's life down its column.

How to read it

Three things to know, and the map reads itself. On your first visit, a guided tour shows the rest on the real map.

One column per team, one row per Worlds

Every team that has played at least one Worlds has its column, from 2011 at the top to the latest edition at the bottom. A row holds the players who actually played that Worlds for the team, not the season's roster. A greyed-out cell: the team was not there, and no roster is guessed.

One thread per player

A thread links a player's appearances, from one team to the next. Its colour gives the role, and switches at its midpoint when the player changes role. Its two ends mark the first and last Worlds; in between, hovering shows where they were in the years without Worlds.

A few signs

Two half-cells: a role shared between two players. An asterisk: an occasional rotation. A dotted thread: no professional game on record over the interval. Above a row: how far the team went.

In figures

Computed when the page opens, from the data files, like the map's statistics.

Method and sources

Each row shows the players who actually played that Worlds for the team, not the season's roster, drawn from a single source: Leaguepedia's game records. Four written rules turn those games into a lineup — who holds a role, when a role is shared, how a player is spelled — and the years outside Worlds come from the same source under a fifth. Nothing is typed in from memory: a script recomputes the whole dataset on every collection run and reports the slightest divergence, which makes it reproducible.

A row's region is the league the team actually played in the year of the edition, not the one its organisation belongs to today. A player shows under the handle they bore that year, without their thread breaking.

Between two Worlds, the map says where a player was: the club, or clubs, where they played most that year, from the same game records. Where no professional game is on record, it says exactly that — never “retired”, never “no team”.

The data is plain JSON files, published with the code and checked on every change; each edition's sources are listed alongside. An error is reported with a source, and never corrected from memory.

Not included yet: MSI, and a player's team-mates during their years outside Worlds.
